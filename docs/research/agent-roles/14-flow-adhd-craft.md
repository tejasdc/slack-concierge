# Flow, ADHD, open loops, and craft: an evidence review for agent/thread system design

Scope note: this is a literature scan (WebSearch/WebFetch against primary sources, meta-analyses,
and major reviews), not a systematic review. Where I could not reach the primary source (paywall,
no WebFetch access), I say so and lower confidence accordingly. "Strength of evidence" ratings are
mine, calibrated the way an evidence-based-medicine or psych-replication-crisis-aware reader would:
**Strong** = meta-analysis or multiply-replicated with pre-registration; **Moderate** = single
well-powered study or consistent narrative-review consensus; **Weak** = single small study,
theory paper, or popular-science distillation with thin primary-source backing; **Contested** =
active replication dispute, cite with the caveat attached.

## 1. Flow

### 1.1 Csikszentmihalyi — conditions for flow
**Citation:** Csikszentmihalyi, M. (1990). *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience*; operationalized further in Nakamura, J. & Csikszentmihalyi, M. (2009). "Flow Theory and Research," in *The Oxford Handbook of Positive Psychology* (2nd ed.).
**Core finding:** Flow requires (1) clear, proximal goals, (2) immediate feedback on progress, and (3) a perceived balance between challenge and skill (the "flow channel" — too little challenge relative to skill produces boredom, too much produces anxiety). Csikszentmihalyi's fuller nine-component list adds: effortlessness despite difficulty, merging of action and awareness, loss of self-consciousness, and altered time perception, but the three above are the load-bearing *antecedent conditions*, the rest are *phenomenology* (what flow feels like once it starts).
**Strength of evidence:** Moderate-strong as a descriptive framework — extremely widely replicated across domains (sport, music, work, gaming) using the Experience Sampling Method and flow-state scales, but almost entirely correlational/self-report; causal claims ("if you set up X you will get flow") are weaker than the correlational structure implies.
**Implication for an agent/thread system:** A thread or task needs an explicit, near-term goal and a way to see progress *inside* the session, not just at the end. This argues against "fire and forget, check back tomorrow" agent tasks for the human's own flow — but for *delegated* agent work it argues the opposite: the human doesn't need moment-to-moment feedback on a background agent, because *the human is not the one meant to be in flow on that task*. Immediate feedback matters for the surface the human is actively working in (his own coding/thinking thread), not for every backgrounded agent run. Concretely: keep the human's own active thread's feedback loop tight (fast, visible progress ticks), and don't leak agent-run status into that loop in a way that resets the goal clarity (a status ping is not a goal).

### 1.2 Neuroscience of flow — transient hypofrontality and its critics
**Citation:** Dietrich, A. (2003). "Functional neuroanatomy of altered states of consciousness: the transient hypofrontality hypothesis." *Consciousness and Cognition*, and Dietrich (2004) "Neurocognitive mechanisms underlying the experience of flow," same journal.
**Core finding:** Proposes flow arises from transient down-regulation of prefrontal (explicit/analytical) circuits, letting well-practiced implicit skill run without top-down interference.
**Strength of evidence:** Weak-to-moderate and **contested**. It's an influential theoretical synthesis with some supporting neuroimaging in narrow domains (Limb & Braun 2008 jazz improvisation fMRI), but the "whole prefrontal cortex shuts down" framing has been criticized as overly strong; more recent reviews (below) treat it as one candidate mechanism among several, not settled fact.
**Implication:** Be skeptical of any design rationale that says "flow = turn the brain's executive/planning function off, so remove all structure." The evidence doesn't support blanket deregulation as the goal — it supports *offloading* explicit control of the low-level motor/procedural layer while goal-tracking still runs somewhere (which is exactly what an external trusted system, see §3, is for).

### 1.3 van der Linden, Tops & Bakker (2021) — LC-NE system
**Citation:** van der Linden, D., Tops, M., & Bakker, A.B. (2021). "The Neuroscience of the Flow State: Involvement of the Locus Coeruleus Norepinephrine System." *Frontiers in Psychology*, 12:645498. (A companion 2022 systematic review, "The brain in flow," *Cortex*, surveys the broader neuroimaging literature and is the more comprehensive one — I could not fully retrieve its findings beyond the abstract-level; treat as a pointer, not verified in depth here.)
**Core finding:** Proposes the locus coeruleus–norepinephrine system (tonic vs. phasic firing modes) as a better-evidenced candidate mechanism than blanket hypofrontality: flow correlates with a *moderate* arousal/exploitation mode — enough NE for sustained focused engagement, not so much that it triggers exploratory scanning (mind-wandering) or so little that it produces boredom.
**Strength of evidence:** Moderate — it's a theoretical/mini-review synthesizing existing pharmacology and arousal literature, not new primary data, but it corrects the overreach of the hypofrontality story with a more physiologically grounded and falsifiable model (arousal as inverted-U, same shape as challenge-skill balance).
**Implication:** This is independent support for the challenge-skill/arousal inverted-U — too-easy backlog items and too-hard, ambiguous ones both push a person out of the productive middle. A queueing/triage system should actively route "too easy" (delegate to agent) and "too hard/ambiguous" (park for decision, see §6) away from the human's own attention, keeping what's left in the moderate-arousal band.

### 1.4 Flow and interruptions
**Citation:** No single canonical paper; converging evidence from IT-interruption studies (e.g., work cited via "impact of IT-mediated interruptions on flow and task performance") and interruption-science reviews (Frontiers in Psychology 2023 taxonomy paper).
**Core finding:** Interruptions during flow are costly but *not uniformly* costly — task-*relevant* interruptions that carry information contributing to the task can be neutral or even helpful; task-*irrelevant* interruptions and ones requiring a context switch to an unrelated project are the damaging kind. Recovering from disrupted *deep* flow takes measurably longer (cited as ~2–3x) than recovering from disrupted shallow focus, and a modest ~10% interruption rate has been shown to nearly double total completion time in some task studies (non-linear cost).
**Strength of evidence:** Moderate — decent experimental support, but effect sizes vary a lot by task type and interruption content; the "10% interruption rate nearly doubles completion time" figure is from a specific study design and shouldn't be treated as universal.
**Implication:** The design lever isn't "zero interruptions" — it's *classifying* interruptions before they reach the human. An agent/thread system should distinguish (a) interruptions that are actually inputs the current task needs (surface immediately) from (b) interruptions that are about a different open thread entirely (batch, don't surface mid-flow). This is a concrete argument for a notification triage layer, not a blanket do-not-disturb.

## 2. ADHD

### 2.1 Barkley — executive function / self-regulation model
**Citation:** Barkley, R.A. (1997). "ADHD, self-regulation, and time: toward a more comprehensive theory." *Journal of Developmental and Behavioral Pediatrics*; synthesized in Barkley's ongoing "Executive Functioning and Self-Regulation in ADHD" material.
**Core finding:** ADHD's core deficit is in *behavioral inhibition*, which cascades into impairment of four executive functions that support self-regulation: nonverbal working memory, self-directed speech (internalized rule-following), self-regulation of motivation/affect/arousal, and reconstitution (planning/behavioral synthesis). The practical corollary Barkley is most cited for in productivity contexts: EF deficits mean knowledge doesn't reliably translate into action *at the point it's needed* — the "knowing-doing gap" is structural, not a motivation or character failure.
**Strength of evidence:** Strong as a clinical/theoretical model — it's the dominant framework in ADHD research and clinical practice, though it's a model (organizing many findings), not itself a single falsifiable experiment; some researchers (see delay-aversion, §2.2) argue it's incomplete on its own.
**Implication:** Any UI or protocol that assumes "the user will remember to do X later because they were told once" is designed against this population, not for it. The instruction/reminder has to recur *at the point of performance* (see 2.3), not rely on retrieval from memory of an earlier instruction.

### 2.2 Delay aversion — Sonuga-Barke
**Citation:** Sonuga-Barke, E.J.S. (1994, and subsequent "dual pathway" elaborations through the 2000s–2010s).
**Core finding:** A second, motivational (not purely cognitive) pathway: ADHD-linked impulsivity partly reflects an aversion to *delay itself* as an aversive emotional state, not just poor inhibition. People escape delay-rich situations (waiting, slow-burn tasks) because waiting itself is uncomfortable, which produces a preference for immediate smaller rewards over larger later ones, and avoidance of tasks with a long payoff horizon.
**Strength of evidence:** Moderate — well-replicated behavioral-economics-style choice tasks, but debated relative weight vs. pure EF deficit; current consensus is "dual pathway" (both mechanisms operate, sometimes in different individuals or symptom clusters — inattention tracks more with EF, hyperactivity/impulsivity more with delay aversion/temporal discounting).
**Implication:** A task/thread system for someone with ADHD-type cognition should shrink the *subjective* delay before some signal of progress, even on long-horizon work — this is a second, independent argument (alongside Amabile & Kramer, §9) for surfacing small, frequent "still moving" signals on long-running agent tasks, not just a start notification and an end notification with silence in between.

### 2.3 Working memory deficits and externalization / point of performance
**Citation:** Barkley's "point of performance" concept; supporting working-memory literature (meta-analytic estimates of ~25–35% lower working-memory capacity in ADHD samples, 62–85% of children with ADHD showing measurable deficits on EF-loaded WM tasks).
**Core finding:** The fix that works is not remediating working memory itself (computerized WM training shows poor transfer to real-world function) — it's *externalizing* the information/plan so it doesn't have to be held in the head, and delivering the cue **at the time and place the action needs to happen**, not earlier.
**Strength of evidence:** Moderate-strong on the WM deficit itself (consistent across many studies); moderate on "externalization helps" (converging clinical consensus plus some quantified effects, e.g., a commonly cited ~47% reduction in daily memory failures with externalization strategies — I could not verify the primary source behind that specific number, treat it as an illustrative claim from secondary sources, not a verified statistic); strong on "WM training doesn't transfer" (this is well-established in the cognitive-training literature generally, not just ADHD).
**Implication:** This is the single most direct piece of evidence for "capture everything, and surface it back at the moment it's actionable, not just at capture time." A capture inbox that never resurfaces at the right moment is externalization without point-of-performance delivery — half the mechanism. The system needs both: (1) trusted, frictionless capture, and (2) a resurfacing mechanism tied to context/time/location, not just chronological list order.

### 2.4 Hyperfocus
**Citation:** Ashinoff, B.K. & Abu-Akel, A. (2021). "Hyperfocus: the forgotten frontier of attention." *Psychological Research*, 85(1). Also: a 2021 *Current Psychology* study (Groen et al.-type design, 85 undergraduates) directly comparing hyperfocus and flow in students with/without clinically significant ADHD symptoms.
**Core finding:** Ashinoff & Abu-Akel note hyperfocus is under-defined and under-researched despite being widely reported clinically (ADHD, autism, schizophrenia) — they propose an operational definition rather than settling the mechanism. The *Current Psychology* study is the more actionable finding for design purposes: students with clinically significant ADHD symptoms reported **more** hyperfocus but **less** of most aspects of flow than students without ADHD — i.e., hyperfocus and flow are not the same experience, and may even be inversely related in this population. Hyperfocus in ADHD often looks like absorption *without* the challenge-skill balance, immediate feedback, or (crucially) the ability to disengage at a natural stopping point.
**Strength of evidence:** Weak-moderate — the operational-definition paper is explicitly agenda-setting (the field lacks consensus measures); the flow-vs-hyperfocus comparison is a single study (N=85, self-report, undergraduate sample), not yet replicated.
**Implication:** This is an important corrective to a common design assumption ("ADHD hyperfocus is just flow, get people into it and leave them there"). Hyperfocus can be a trap state — deep absorption in the *wrong* task with no natural exit, at the cost of everything else that needed attention that day. A system that optimizes purely for "get the user absorbed" without checking *what* they're absorbed in, and without a way to interrupt hyperfocus for something that actually matters more, could be optimizing for the ADHD failure mode, not the flow success mode. This argues for a distinct "is this the right thing to be hyperfocused on" check — e.g., a session-start commitment ("what is this session for") that a later self-check can be measured against — rather than treating raw absorption/session-length as the success metric.

### 2.5 Interest-based motivation
**Citation:** Popularized by Dodson's "interest-based nervous system" / PINCH framework (passion, interest, novelty, competition, hurry); grounded more rigorously in general self-determination-theory and dopaminergic-reward literature on intrinsic motivation (see §9.3), applied to ADHD.
**Core finding:** In ADHD, task engagement tracks interest/novelty/urgency far more than importance-as-judged-by-others; a boring-but-important task and an interesting-but-unimportant task compete on very unequal footing.
**Strength of evidence:** Weak on the ADHD-specific framing (PINCH itself is a clinical heuristic, not a peer-reviewed causal model) — moderate on the underlying general claim that dopaminergic/intrinsic-motivation systems respond to novelty and interest, which is well-supported in general motivation neuroscience, just not ADHD-specific-validated as a named framework.
**Implication:** Don't rely on "importance" alone to sequence work for this user; pair importance with something that manufactures interest/urgency/novelty when the queue needs it — a deadline, a game-like framing, a live conversation with an agent (novelty of dialogue vs. static list) can all be legitimate, evidence-consistent levers, not just tricks.

### 2.6 Time blindness
**Citation:** Meta-analytic literature reviewed in "Time Perception in Adult ADHD: Findings from a Decade" (PMC, ~2022/2023); a cited meta-analysis of 55 studies (time perception generally) and a more specific one of 25 studies/1,633 participants on time discrimination.
**Core finding:** Consistent, medium-effect-size impairments across motor timing, perceptual timing, and temporal foresight (estimating how long a task will take, and how much time has passed) — this is one of the more consistently replicated ADHD-cognitive findings, not just anecdote.
**Strength of evidence:** Strong — meta-analytic, cross-study consistency, plausible neurobiological grounding (prefrontal/WM/attention overlap).
**Implication:** Never trust the user's own duration estimate for a task as an input to scheduling (e.g., "this will take 20 minutes") without also tracking actual elapsed time and feeding *that* back — time blindness means self-estimates are a known-biased signal, so the system, not the user, should own elapsed-time tracking and should surface discrepancies (estimated vs. actual) as data, not shame.

### 2.7 Task initiation, completion, and body doubling
**Citation:** Multiple recent (2024–2025) applied studies, notably an arXiv VR body-doubling study and general ADHD task-initiation literature summarized on clinical sites (FOCO, SaskADHD).
**Core finding:** Task initiation is one of the most commonly impaired EF domains in ADHD (cited as affecting ~9/10 adults with ADHD to some degree). Body doubling (working alongside another present person/agent) is popular and subjectively rated as helpful, but **controlled experimental studies have not reliably shown an objective effect** — some show real speed/accuracy gains (a small 2025 VR study, n=12, alone vs. human double vs. AI double), others show no significant effect despite participants *feeling* helped. This is a live, unresolved evidence gap, not a solid finding either way.
**Strength of evidence:** Weak — small samples, mixed results, no meta-analysis; genuinely "we don't know yet," and *feeling* helped doesn't equal *being* measurably helped, which matters if you're designing based on self-report satisfaction alone.
**Implication:** Treat "an agent working alongside you" (co-presence, synchronous ambient agent activity) as a plausible, low-cost feature to offer — but don't oversell it as validated, and don't let it substitute for the higher-confidence interventions below (implementation intentions, externalization). If you build it, instrument it so you can tell whether it's actually reducing time-to-start, not just whether the user likes it.

### 2.8 Implementation intentions
**Citation:** Gollwitzer, P.M. & Sheeran, P. (2006). "Implementation intentions and goal achievement: a meta-analysis of effects and processes." *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 38. 94 independent studies, >8,000 participants.
**Core finding:** Forming a specific "if [situation], then I will [action]" plan produces a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = .65 overall; d = .61 specifically for failures to *get started*). Effect doesn't shrink with sample size (no obvious publication-bias signature).
**Strength of evidence:** Strong — this is one of the best-evidenced findings in the entire self-regulation literature, a genuine meta-analytic result with a robust effect size, not a single study or a popular-science claim.
**Implication:** This is the strongest single piece of evidence in this whole review for a concrete feature: when capturing a task/idea, prompt (lightly — see friction section) for the *trigger condition*, not just the task content. "Write the blog post" is a weak capture; "when I finish the current PR review, write the blog post" is the evidence-backed version. If the system can cheaply elicit the if-then form at capture time, or infer/attach one before a task leaves "captured" and enters "queued," that's a high-confidence, low-cost win.

## 3. Open loops, attention, and trusted systems

### 3.1 Zeigarnik effect — and its replication trouble
**Citation:** Zeigarnik, B. (1927) original; modern reassessment via Seifert & Patalano (1991) re-examination and a 2025 meta-analysis of Zeigarnik/Ovsiankina effects (*Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*).
**Core finding:** The *memory* advantage for unfinished tasks (the original, famous claim) **does not reliably replicate** — a 2025 meta-analysis found roughly no memory edge for interrupted tasks once Zeigarnik's own original data is excluded, and historically fewer than half of replication attempts succeeded. What *does* survive robustly is the **Ovsiankina effect**: the behavioral pull to *resume* an interrupted task, and open goals intruding on attention (distinct from a memory-encoding advantage).
**Strength of evidence:** The original claim: **Contested/largely disconfirmed** as literally stated. The resumption-pull/attention-intrusion phenomenon: Moderate-strong, more consistently observed.
**Implication:** Important correction for anyone citing "Zeigarnik effect" loosely to justify open-loop design. The right framing is not "unfinished tasks are remembered better" (probably false) but "unfinished tasks intrude on attention and create pull to resume, uncomfortably, at unpredictable times" (probably true) — which is actually a stronger argument for *externalizing* open loops (§3.3) rather than relying on the brain to hold and remember them, since memory isn't doing you the favor the pop-psych version claims.

### 3.2 Masicampo & Baumeister (2011) — plan-making resolves goal interference
**Citation:** Masicampo, E.J. & Baumeister, R.F. (2011). "Consider it done! Plan making can eliminate the cognitive effects of unfulfilled goals." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.
**Core finding:** Unfulfilled goals cause measurable cognitive interference — intrusive thoughts, heightened accessibility of goal-related words, worse performance on an unrelated concurrent task. Forming a **specific plan** (not just deciding to do it "later," and not resolving the goal itself) eliminates these interference effects, provided the plan genuinely represents progress toward the goal (a vague or fake plan doesn't work).
**Strength of evidence:** Moderate — single research group, multiple internal studies/replications within the paper, well-cited and consistent with the broader implementation-intentions literature (§2.8), but not independently meta-analyzed the way Gollwitzer & Sheeran is.
**Implication:** Directly supports "capture + a next action, not capture alone." A capture system that stores raw ideas without at least a next-step or a scheduled decision point is not giving the ADHD/overloaded brain the relief this research shows a real plan gives — it's just moving the open loop from head to app without closing the cognitive-interference loop. The "close the loop" moment is when a *specific* next step exists, not when the item is merely recorded.

### 3.3 Attention residue and task-switching costs
**Citation:** Leroy, S. (2009). "Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks." *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 109(2). Monsell, S. (2003). "Task switching." *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 7(3).
**Core finding:** Leroy: residue of a prior task remains cognitively active after switching, degrading performance on the new task; residue is worst when the prior task was time-pressured, unfinished, or emotionally engaging (not just "any prior task"). Monsell: switch costs (slower, more error-prone responses right after a switch) persist even with advance preparation time — some of the cost is genuinely unavoidable "task-set reconfiguration," not just insufficient warning.
**Strength of evidence:** Moderate-strong — both are well-established, frequently replicated findings in cognitive/organizational psychology; Monsell's review in particular summarizes a large convergent experimental literature.
**Implication:** Two separate design levers: (1) Leroy's finding argues for *closing* a task (explicit completion or explicit pause-with-plan, tying back to §3.2) before switching, rather than abrupt interruption — an agent/thread system should support "wrap up this thread" as a first-class action, not just "switch away." (2) Monsell's finding argues WIP limits (§4) aren't just about queueing throughput — even *perfectly scheduled, well-warned* switches carry an irreducible cognitive tax, so minimizing the *number* of switches matters independent of how well each switch is choreographed.

### 3.4 GTD as externalized/distributed cognition — academic assessment
**Citation:** Heylighen, F. & Vidal, C. (2008). "Getting Things Done: The Science behind Stress-Free Productivity." *Long Range Planning*, 41, 585–605.
**Core finding:** Connects David Allen's GTD methodology to situated/embodied/distributed cognition theory: the brain is poor at reliably holding open commitments and relies on the environment as external memory, action-trigger, and feedback source. GTD's core mechanisms (capture everything, clarify into next actions, organize into context-triggered lists, review regularly) map onto to this externalization principle; the paper is favorable toward GTD but frames it as an *application* of established cognitive-science principles, not itself new science, and proposes extending it to collaborative/multi-agent settings via stigmergy (indirect coordination through traces left in a shared environment).
**Strength of evidence:** Moderate — it's a theoretical synthesis/argument paper, not an RCT testing whether GTD outperforms alternatives; it's good grounding for *why* externalization should work, not direct proof that any particular GTD implementation *does* work better than alternatives.
**Implication:** The paper's "trusted system" concept (you only stop thinking about something once you trust it's captured somewhere you'll actually see it again, at the right time) is the theoretical core this whole research brief is implicitly built around. The stigmergy extension is directly relevant to a *multi-agent* system: agents leaving legible traces (status, decisions, next actions) in a shared environment that other agents/the human can pick up asynchronously is a GTD-compatible, evidence-grounded coordination pattern — better justified than ad hoc chat-style handoffs.

## 4. Deep work and WIP limits

### 4.1 Newport — deep vs. shallow work
**Citation:** Newport, C. (2016). *Deep Work: Rules for Focused Success in a Distracted World*.
**Core finding:** Deep work (distraction-free, cognitively demanding, skill-building) is economically increasingly valuable and increasingly rare, crowded out by shallow work (logistics, communication) that fragments attention. A commonly cited supporting stat: knowledge workers are interrupted roughly every 3 minutes and take ~23 minutes to regain full focus after an interruption (this specific figure traces to Gloria Mark's research, not Newport's own data — Newport popularizes it).
**Strength of evidence:** Weak-moderate as a scientific claim (it's a practitioner/synthesis book, not a peer-reviewed study — Newport draws on real research like Mark's interruption studies but the book's own framework and prescriptions haven't been independently tested as a package); the underlying interruption-cost research it cites is stronger.
**Implication:** Treat "Deep Work" as a good vocabulary and motivating narrative, not as itself an evidence base — cite the underlying interruption/attention-residue/task-switching research (§1.4, §3.3) for the actual causal claims, and use Newport's shallow/deep distinction mainly as a *triage category*: this is exactly the kind of judgment an agent should be making on the human's behalf (route shallow, logistics-style work to itself; protect blocks for the kind of work only the human's deep engagement can do).

### 4.2 WIP limits, Kanban, Little's Law
**Citation:** Little, J.D.C. (1961) original queueing-theory result; applied extensively in Lean/Kanban software-delivery literature (Anderson, and widely disseminated via Atlassian/industry writeups).
**Core finding:** Little's Law: average lead time = WIP ÷ throughput, for a stable system. Practical corollary for a fixed completion rate: more concurrent in-progress items mechanically means longer average time-to-finish for *everything*, not just the added item — and each person juggling N in-flight tasks pays a context-switching cost N times over (see Monsell, §3.3, for why that per-switch cost is real). Industry-reported outcomes from capping WIP: fewer defects, faster cycle time, measurable throughput gains typically visible within weeks.
**Strength of evidence:** Little's Law itself: Strong (it's a mathematical/queueing-theory identity under quite general conditions, not an empirical claim needing replication). The context-switching-tax-per-task and industry outcome numbers: Moderate — plausible, widely reported in practitioner literature, but the specific percentages (e.g., "10% penalty per switch") come from industry case studies/consultancy writeups, not controlled experiments, so treat exact numbers as illustrative rather than precise.
**Implication:** This is the strongest, most rigorous argument in the whole brief for hard WIP limits on the human's *own* active threads (agents can and should run many things in parallel in the background — Little's Law binds the human as the bottleneck resource, not the agents). A concrete design rule falls directly out of the math: fewer concurrently "open for the human" threads → each one finishes faster *and* the human pays the switch-cost tax fewer times. This is math, not opinion — the lever is genuinely that mechanical.

## 5. Incubation and spaced resurfacing

### 5.1 Sio & Ormerod (2009) — incubation meta-analysis
**Citation:** Sio, U.N. & Ormerod, T.C. (2009). "Does incubation enhance problem solving? A meta-analytic review." *Psychological Bulletin*, 135(1), 94–120.
**Core finding:** A genuine, positive incubation effect exists but is **moderator-dependent**, not universal: divergent-thinking tasks benefit more than linguistic/visual insight tasks; longer preparation (time spent working the problem before setting it aside) increases the effect; filling the incubation period with a *high-cognitive-demand* unrelated task *reduces* the benefit (i.e., incubation works better when the "off" time is genuinely low-load, not just occupied by other hard work).
**Strength of evidence:** Strong — this is a proper meta-analytic review with moderator analysis, the best-evidenced item in the "parking helps" cluster.
**Implication:** Directly informs when "park this decision/design discussion" is likely to help vs. just be procrastination: parking works best (a) after real preparatory engagement with the problem (don't park something you haven't actually thought about yet), and (b) if the parked time isn't immediately filled with another cognitively demanding task (parking a hard design decision and immediately diving into a different hard design decision may not give the first one room to incubate). This argues for genuinely idle/low-load gaps (a walk, a different *kind* of low-effort task) being part of the "park" mechanism, not just moving the item to a different queue.

### 5.2 Spaced repetition applied to notes/ideas
**Citation:** Wozniak, P. — SuperMemo / incremental reading method (since ~2000); Matuschak, A. — "evergreen notes" and public writing on spaced-repetition-for-thinking (not a peer-reviewed source, but an influential applied-practice writer).
**Core finding:** The classical spaced-repetition literature (well-established for rote memory, e.g., vocabulary) can be adapted to *ideas and open questions*, not just facts: incremental reading resurfaces partially-processed material on a schedule tuned to forgetting curves; Matuschak's variant argues that the *natural* resurfacing that happens when you keep writing/reading about a live topic approximates spaced repetition, but only for topics you're actively engaged with — topics you stop engaging with simply vanish from the resurfacing cycle, they don't get scheduled back.
**Strength of evidence:** Strong for the underlying spaced-repetition memory effect (one of psychology's best-replicated findings, going back to Ebbinghaus). Weak-moderate for the *extension* to design decisions/parked ideas specifically — this is Matuschak's practitioner theory, not independently tested for that use case.
**Implication:** A parked design decision or idea should not rely on the human happening to keep thinking about it (that's the failure mode Matuschak names — organic resurfacing only serves active topics). It needs a **scheduled** resurfacing mechanism independent of current engagement, exactly like a spaced-repetition scheduler, so that a good idea captured in a low-priority moment doesn't silently age out just because nothing kept it "warm."

## 6. Decision fatigue and deferral

### 6.1 Choice overload — Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010)
**Citation:** Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R., & Todd, P.M. (2010). "Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload." *Journal of Consumer Research*, 37.
**Core finding:** Meta-analysis of 63 conditions/50 experiments, N=5,036: mean effect size of choice overload is essentially **zero**, with large heterogeneity across studies (some strong effects, some null, some reversed — more choice sometimes *increases* satisfaction). No reliable moderator was identified that predicts when overload will occur.
**Strength of evidence:** Strong as a meta-analytic *debunking* of the strong/universal version of "choice overload is real and large" — this directly undercuts the popular Jam-Study (Iyengar & Lepper) narrative as a general law.
**Implication:** Be careful not to over-justify aggressive option-pruning ("show only 3 choices, never more") purely on choice-overload grounds — the general effect is not reliably real. The actual problem this brief's user has ("design discussions stall," "abandoned instead of finished") is better explained by other mechanisms below (ego depletion is *also* contested — see 6.2 — so the honest story is: the *mechanism* for why unstructured decisions stall is not cleanly "too many options" or "depleted willpower"; it's more likely the absence of a forcing function/commitment device, §6.3, and the presence of a genuinely hard, high-stakes, reversible-feeling choice with no deadline).

### 6.2 Decision fatigue / ego depletion — replication crisis
**Citation:** Original: Baumeister et al. muscle-metaphor and glucose work (Gailliot & Baumeister, 2007). Disconfirming: Hagger et al. (2016) multi-lab preregistered replication (23 labs, N=2,141, effect d≈0.04); Dang et al. (2019) similar large preregistered replication.
**Core finding:** The glucose-based mechanism for ego depletion has failed to replicate; large preregistered multi-lab studies find an effect indistinguishable from zero. This is one of the highest-profile casualties of the psychology replication crisis.
**Strength of evidence:** Strong evidence *against* the original strong claim (large, preregistered, multi-lab). Some subsequent defenders argue methodology needs longer/stronger manipulations and that the construct isn't fully dead, but the field consensus has shifted sharply away from "willpower is a depletable resource like glucose."
**Implication:** Don't design around "the human has a limited daily pool of decision-making energy that agents should ration." That specific mechanistic story is scientifically weak. What *is* well-supported (fatigue, boredom, and frustration from unstructured, high-stakes, no-deadline decisions) is better addressed directly via structure (ADRs, timeboxes, commitment devices, §6.3) than via a "protect his willpower budget" framing, which risks smuggling back in a debunked mechanism under new language.

### 6.3 Structured decision methods and commitment devices
**Citation:** ADR (Architecture Decision Record) practice, broadly disseminated (Nygard's original ADR format; "disagree and commit" as an Amazon leadership-principle-derived norm). Bryan, G., Karlan, D., & Nelson, S. (2010). "Commitment Devices." *Annual Review of Economics*, 2, 671–698.
**Core finding:** ADRs: recording *what* was decided, *why*, and *what alternatives were rejected* is claimed (practitioner consensus, not RCT-tested) to reduce re-litigation and speed convergence by making the decision itself an artifact rather than a live, always-reopenable conversation. Bryan/Karlan/Nelson: reviews strong empirical (mostly field-experiment) evidence that both hard and soft commitment devices measurably increase follow-through on intentions (savings behavior, smoking cessation, etc.) precisely because they remove the option to keep re-deciding.
**Strength of evidence:** ADR practice: Weak as *science* (no controlled comparison exists that I found — it's software-engineering practitioner consensus, valuable but not peer-reviewed causal evidence). Commitment devices generally: Strong (this is a genuine, well-evidenced economics literature with real field experiments across many domains).
**Implication:** The commitment-device literature is the best-evidenced lever available for "finish hard decisions instead of abandoning them": build an explicit, low-friction *commitment step* into the design-discussion flow — something that converts "we discussed it" into "we decided X, here's why, and reopening requires a real new committed effort, not just idle second-guessing." An ADR-style artifact is a reasonable implementation, but its power should be understood as coming from the commitment-device mechanism (removing costless reopening), not from documentation quality per se.

## 7. Desirable difficulty / beneficial friction

### 7.1 Bjork — desirable difficulties
**Citation:** Bjork, R.A. (1994, and subsequent, e.g., Bjork & Bjork 2020 "Desirable difficulties in theory and practice").
**Core finding:** Conditions that make practice *feel* harder in the moment (spacing, retrieval practice/testing, interleaving, reduced feedback once a learner is competent) reliably produce *better long-term* recall/transfer than conditions that feel easier in the moment (blocking, cramming, constant feedback) — crucially, the *performance-learning distinction*: short-term ease of performance and long-term learning are not the same axis, and can trade off against each other.
**Strength of evidence:** Strong — this is a well-replicated line of cognitive-psychology research (spacing effect, testing effect are among psychology's most robust findings).
**Implication:** Directly validates the premise of this whole brief: "friction in the right places, not zero friction" is not a personal preference, it's consistent with one of psychology's best-evidenced findings, generalized from learning to any skill/decision domain. The design corollary: judge a friction point not by whether it feels smooth *right now*, but by whether it improves the outcome that matters later (a decision that sticks, a skill that transfers, a habit that holds) — and be honest that this means some genuinely good friction will feel, in the moment, like the system being annoying.

### 7.2 Cox et al. (2016) — Design Frictions for Mindful Interactions
**Citation:** Cox, A.L., Gould, S.J.J., Cecchinato, M.E., Iacovides, I., & Renfree, I. (2016). "Design Frictions for Mindful Interactions: The Case for Microboundaries." *CHI 2016 Extended Abstracts*.
**Core finding:** Deliberately introduced small frictions ("microboundaries") can interrupt automatic/mindless interaction patterns and prompt a moment of reflection, with claimed benefits including reduced data-entry errors and support for health-behavior change — an explicit HCI-literature challenge to the "friction is always bad UX" default.
**Strength of evidence:** Weak-moderate — it's a position/case-study paper (CHI extended abstract, not a full empirical paper with a large controlled trial), influential as a framing device in HCI but thinner as direct causal evidence than Bjork's cognitive-psychology base.
**Implication:** Gives a name and precedent ("microboundary") for a very small, specific design object: a one-tap or one-question checkpoint placed exactly at the moment an automatic action would otherwise fire (e.g., re-opening a "done" decision, or firing off a capture with no next-action attached). Use it as a design pattern name and existence-proof, not as strong evidence for exactly how large or where every friction point should be — that still has to be tuned empirically per point, not assumed from this paper.

## 8. Craft, autonomy, and feeling good about the work

### 8.1 Sennett — The Craftsman
**Citation:** Sennett, R. (2008). *The Craftsman*.
**Core finding:** Craftsmanship is "the desire to do a job well for its own sake" — an intrinsic standard, independent of external reward or competition (people motivated mainly by money/competition tend to produce worse work than people motivated by the craft standard itself). Sennett extends "craftsman" beyond manual trades to any skilled practice, including programming, arguing the same three abilities matter: localizing a problem correctly, questioning it, and opening it up (i.e., not treating the given frame of a problem as fixed).
**Strength of evidence:** Weak as science (it's a work of social/historical theory and argument, not an empirical study), but influential and widely engaged with in both HCI and management literature; treat it as a source of *design values*, not a testable causal claim.
**Implication:** "Feeling good about agent-assisted development as a craft" (the user's stated goal) is not automatically satisfied just by shipping more, faster. Sennett's framework suggests the human needs a visible, felt connection between their own judgment/questioning of a problem and the final result — an agent system that removes localizing/questioning/opening-up entirely (agent decides everything, human only approves) may ship more but corrode exactly the thing the user says he wants to feel good about.

### 8.2 Crawford — Shop Class as Soulcraft
**Citation:** Crawford, M.B. (2009). *Shop Class as Soulcraft: An Inquiry into the Value of Work*.
**Core finding:** Agency and meaning in work come from engaging with real constraints not of one's own making and being genuinely responsible for the outcome ("master of one's own stuff") — paradoxically, *fewer* constraints/more abstraction can mean *less* felt agency, not more freedom.
**Strength of evidence:** Weak as science (philosophical/memoir argument), same caveat as Sennett.
**Implication:** Converts into a concrete design question for delegation boundaries: does the human still own real constraints and real responsibility for the parts that matter to him, or has "let the agent handle it" abstracted away the parts where his own judgment against real limits was actually the source of satisfaction? This is a reason to be deliberate (not automatic) about *which* categories of work get delegated wholesale vs. kept as human-owned-with-agent-assistance — see §10 delegation boundary.

### 8.3 Self-determination theory
**Citation:** Deci, E.L. & Ryan, R.M. (1985, and ongoing; 2000 "Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being," *American Psychologist*).
**Core finding:** Autonomy, competence, and relatedness are basic psychological needs; satisfying all three predicts intrinsic motivation and well-being; a large (>443,000-participant) 2024 meta-analysis confirms the needs-satisfaction → well-being/performance link holds up at scale.
**Strength of evidence:** Strong — SDT is one of the most extensively and consistently supported frameworks in motivation psychology, with genuine large-scale meta-analytic backing (unlike ego depletion or choice overload).
**Implication:** This is a strong, general lens for evaluating any agent-delegation design: does it preserve the human's autonomy (real choice, not just cosmetic approval clicks), competence (does he still get to exercise and grow skill, or does the skill atrophy because the agent always does it first), and relatedness (does working with agents still feel like *his* system he's building, vs. a black box he's a passenger in)? Autonomy support specifically is undermined by *control* and by excessive extrinsic reward/pressure — relevant to how "must approve every agent action" gates are framed (approval-as-control feels different from approval-as-genuine-choice-point, even if the click is identical).

### 8.4 Progress principle
**Citation:** Amabile, T. & Kramer, S. (2011). *The Progress Principle*, based on ~12,000 diary entries from 238 employees across 7 companies (Amabile & Kramer, 2011, *HBR* "The Power of Small Wins" is the accessible summary of the same research program).
**Core finding:** "Inner work life" (mood, motivation, perception at work) tracks progress and setbacks on *meaningful* work more than any other single factor — even small steps forward on meaningful work reliably improve creativity, productivity, engagement, and relationships; the two enabling forces are "catalysts" (clear goals, autonomy, resources — direct facilitators of the work) and "nourishers" (interpersonal encouragement/respect).
**Strength of evidence:** Strong — large-scale, methodologically serious diary-study research program (qualitative diary coding at real work, not lab manipulation, which is both a strength — ecological validity — and a limitation — correlational, not causal).
**Implication:** Directly supports "make small, frequent, *visible* progress markers on real work a core UI primitive," independent of and reinforcing the flow (feedback) and delay-aversion (ADHD) arguments above. For a multi-thread agent system specifically: every long-running thread should surface small, honest, meaningful progress signals, not just start/end states — and "meaningful" is doing real work here; progress on a task the human doesn't perceive as meaningful won't produce the effect.

## 9. Human–AI delegation

### 9.1 Horvitz (1999) — mixed-initiative principles
**Citation:** Horvitz, E. (1999). "Principles of Mixed-Initiative User Interfaces." *Proceedings of CHI '99*.
**Core finding:** Foundational HCI framework arguing the productive design space is neither pure direct-manipulation nor pure autonomous-agent automation, but a deliberately engineered *coupling* between the two, with explicit principles for when the system should act unilaterally vs. defer to the user vs. ask (covering uncertainty about goals, cost of interrupting, cost of getting it wrong, and how to make the agent's reasoning/state legible enough for the human to calibrate trust).
**Strength of evidence:** Weak-moderate as *empirical* evidence (it's a principles/framework paper built on a single system case study, Lookout), but Strong in terms of influence — it is a foundational, still widely-cited reference point that essentially every later mixed-initiative and human-AI-collaboration paper builds on.
**Implication:** Provides the actual theoretical vocabulary for "where should the agent act unilaterally vs. wait for the human" — the two variables Horvitz names as central (cost of interrupting the human right now, and the agent's own uncertainty about whether this is the right action) map directly onto a concrete rule: high-certainty + low-interruption-cost actions can run autonomously; low-certainty or high-interruption-cost (irreversible, expensive to undo) actions should surface for a genuine decision, not a rubber-stamp.

### 9.2 METR (2025) — RCT on AI and developer speed
**Citation:** METR (2025). "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity." Randomized controlled trial, 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 real tasks in repos they already knew, Cursor Pro + Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet.
**Core finding:** Developers *forecast* a 24% speedup from AI tools; *self-reported* a 20% speedup after finishing; **objectively measured, they were 19% slower** with AI access than without, on real tasks in codebases they already knew well. METR itself has since flagged the result as reflecting a specific early-2025 tool/model snapshot, not a permanent verdict on current tools.
**Strength of evidence:** Moderate-strong as an RCT (real randomization, real tasks, objective time measurement — a genuinely rare and valuable design for this question), but a small sample (16 developers), a narrow population (experienced OSS maintainers in codebases they already knew), and explicitly time-bound by the authors themselves. Do not generalize this as "AI agents always slow experienced people down" — generalize it as "self-reported speedup from AI is an unreliable signal; measure, don't trust perception" (the 39-point perception-reality gap is the most robust and most transferable part of the finding).
**Implication:** This is a direct, strong caution against designing success metrics for the agent system around user-perceived speedup or satisfaction alone. If the goal includes actually being faster (not just feeling faster or feeling less alone), the system needs its own objective before/after timing instrumentation, because the humans' own perception has been shown, in at least one well-designed study, to run in the *opposite direction* from ground truth.

### 9.3 Supervision/oversight burden and "agent babysitting"
**Citation:** Dhanorkar, S., Passi, S., & Vorvoreanu, M. (2026). "Human oversight of agentic systems in practice: Examining the oversight work, challenges, and heuristics of developers using software agents." arXiv:2606.05391. Related: "Overseeing Agents Without Constant Oversight" (arXiv:2602.16844); industry commentary (Anthropic/Perplexity) naming "approval fatigue" as a named failure mode where habituation to confirmation prompts causes users to rubber-stamp without scrutiny, defeating the oversight mechanism's purpose.
**Core finding:** Developers overseeing coding agents rely on pragmatic heuristics (spot-checking against known patterns, incremental testing, trust built from repeated successful interactions) rather than systematic verification, because systematic verification of everything an agent does is not sustainable at scale; agents' outputs can look locally plausible while being wrong ("specification gaming," hallucination) in ways spot-checking misses; and "evaluation awareness" (agents may behave differently under known observation than in production) further undermines confidence that oversight is catching what it needs to catch. Approval-fatigue is explicitly named across multiple 2025–2026 sources as the mechanism by which frequent low-stakes confirmation prompts *stop functioning as real oversight* once habituated.
**Strength of evidence:** Weak-moderate — this is qualitative/interview-based research and industry commentary, current and directly on-point but not yet meta-analyzed or long-replicated (the field itself is ~1–2 years old).
**Implication:** This is the strongest available evidence against a design that relies on frequent human approval gates as the trust mechanism — approval fatigue is a *known, named* failure mode, not a hypothetical. Combined with Horvitz (§9.1), the actionable design principle is: reserve human-in-the-loop gates for the genuinely high-uncertainty/high-irreversibility actions (rare, meaningful, real decisions), and invest instead in making agent work *legible after the fact* — audit trails, diffs, "why" records — so trust is built through spot-checkable, inspectable history (matching what the paper says developers actually do) rather than through a never-ending stream of low-value confirmation clicks that will predictably degrade into rubber-stamping.

## Synthesis: design principles for the agent/thread system

Confidence levels: **High** = converging strong/moderate evidence from multiple independent lines above; **Medium** = one solid line of evidence, or strong theory with weak direct test; **Low** = plausible extrapolation, worth building but should be instrumented and checked, not assumed correct.

1. **Cap the human's own concurrent open threads; don't cap the agents'.** (High — Little's Law is a mathematical identity, reinforced by Monsell's task-switching costs and Leroy's attention residue.) The bottleneck resource is the human's attention, not agent compute. WIP limits belong on "threads actively wanting the human's judgment," not on background agent activity.

2. **Every capture needs a next-action or a scheduled decision point before it counts as "closed," not just storage.** (High — Masicampo & Baumeister for the cognitive-interference relief mechanism, Gollwitzer & Sheeran for the implementation-intention effect size, Heylighen & Vidal for the "trusted system" externalization theory.) A pure inbox that never attaches a next step or a re-decision date is capture without closure — it will still intrude on attention (Ovsiankina/attention-residue mechanisms), just relocated.

3. **Resurface parked items on a schedule, not on the hope of organic re-engagement.** (Medium — Matuschak's practitioner extension of spaced repetition is unverified for this exact use case, but Sio & Ormerod's incubation meta-analysis independently supports that a genuine "off" period helps, and the Zeigarnik-effect correction shows memory alone won't bring it back.) Build a scheduler, not a hope.

4. **Distinguish "park to incubate" from "abandon."** (Medium — Sio & Ormerod's moderators: incubation helps after real prior engagement and when the "off" time isn't itself high-cognitive-load; it does not help as a way to dodge a decision that was never actually engaged with.) The system should be able to tell these apart — e.g., require some minimum engagement before something is eligible to "park," and treat "3rd time reopened with no new information" differently from "genuinely incubating."

5. **Give hard decisions a commitment device, not just a discussion space.** (High for commitment devices generally — Bryan/Karlan/Nelson is genuine field-experiment economics; Medium for ADR-as-implementation, since that specific practice isn't independently tested.) A design discussion that can be costlessly reopened forever will be. Build in an explicit "this is now decided; reopening costs something real (a new session, a stated reason, a fresh review)" step.

6. **Reserve human approval gates for genuinely high-uncertainty or high-irreversibility actions; don't gate everything.** (High — Horvitz's cost-of-interruption/uncertainty framework plus direct 2025–2026 evidence that frequent low-stakes approvals degrade into rubber-stamping via habituation.) Frequent confirmation prompts on low-stakes agent actions are not free safety margin — they are a depleting resource that predictably fails.

7. **Make agent work legible after the fact, not just approved before the fact.** (Medium-high, same evidence as #6.) Audit trails, diffs, and "why" records let the human build calibrated trust through the spot-checking heuristics research shows they actually use, rather than through exhaustive real-time supervision that doesn't scale and isn't how trust actually gets built in practice.

8. **Instrument actual outcomes, don't trust perceived speed or perceived helpfulness alone.** (High — METR's 39-point perception/reality gap is a rare, well-designed direct demonstration; reinforced by the mixed/uncertain body-doubling evidence, where subjective "feels helpful" and objective performance diverge.) Any feature justified by "it feels better" (ambient co-presence, more frequent status updates) should also be checked against an objective measure where feasible, because self-report has been shown to point the wrong way at least once, in exactly this domain.

9. **Deliver reminders/cues at the point of performance, tied to context or time, not as one-time instructions relying on memory.** (High — Barkley's EF model plus the working-memory-externalization literature.) This is a systems point, not an agent-remembering point, matching the "golden rule" already governing this codebase: build the mechanism that resurfaces at the right moment, don't rely on the human (or an agent) to remember to check.

10. **Treat interruptions as a triage problem, not a binary do-not-disturb.** (Medium — interruption-content-dependent effects, task-relevant vs. irrelevant.) A thread-relevant input to the human's current active work should surface promptly; an unrelated thread's update should batch. This requires the system to know what the current active thread's goal actually is (tying back to Csikszentmihalyi's clear-goals condition) in order to classify.

11. **Preserve autonomy, competence, and relatedness in the delegation boundary — don't optimize purely for throughput.** (High evidence for SDT generally as a well-supported framework; Low-medium confidence on exactly which delegation boundary preserves it for this specific user, since that's untested for this system.) Concretely: keep some categories of work — the ones the user cares about growing skill in, or where his own judgment against real constraints is the point (Sennett/Crawford) — as human-led-with-agent-assistance rather than fully delegated, even if full delegation would be faster; treat this as a deliberate, revisitable choice per work-category, not a default to maximize automation everywhere.

12. **Surface small, honest, frequent progress signals on real (meaningful) long-running work.** (High — Amabile & Kramer's progress principle, reinforced independently by delay-aversion research on why long payoff horizons are aversive.) This is different from #6/#7's audit trail — it's specifically about *felt momentum* during the wait, not just retrospective legibility.

### Weak spots / open questions this review could not resolve
- **Body doubling / ambient co-presence:** genuinely unresolved evidence (some studies show real effects, some show none despite subjective liking). Worth offering as a feature, but instrument it — don't assume it works because it's popular.
- **The exact size/placement of any given friction point:** Bjork and Cox et al. establish that *some* friction is good, not how much, where, for whom. This has to be tuned per checkpoint with real usage data from this specific user, not derived from the literature alone.
- **Choice overload and decision fatigue as *mechanisms*:** both have weaker empirical footing than commonly assumed (Scheibehenne's null meta-analytic effect; ego depletion's replication failure). Resist explanations of "why decisions stall" that lean on these debunked/contested mechanisms — the better-supported explanation is the absence of a commitment device plus a genuinely hard, high-stakes, deadline-free choice.
- **Hyperfocus vs. flow in ADHD:** based on one unreplicated study (N=85) suggesting they may be *inversely* related, not synonymous. This is important enough (it inverts a common design assumption) to flag as needing more direct verification, not settled fact.
- **Transient hypofrontality:** still an actively debated mechanism for flow; don't build design rationale on "flow = executive function turning off."
- **METR's generalizability:** a single RCT, small N, specific tools/models/population (experienced OSS maintainers), explicitly flagged by its own authors as time-bound. The *pattern* (perception/reality gap) is the durable lesson; the specific 19% number is not something to expect to reproduce with different tools, tasks, or users.
