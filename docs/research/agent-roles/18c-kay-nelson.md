## ALAN KAY

### 1. "Personal Dynamic Media" (1977, with Adele Goldberg)

**Primary source, full text read:** *Computer* 10(3):31–41, March 1977; reprinted in *The New
Media Reader* (MIT Press), pp. 393–404. PDF: https://augmentingcognition.com/assets/Kay1977.pdf
(also indexed at https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Personal-Dynamic-Media-Kay-Goldberg/b37b312c7f59dc332799b73271690dcf8a567139
and https://www.newmediareader.com/book_samples/nmr-26-kay.pdf)

**Opening framing — dynamic media used by "human beings of all ages":**
> "The Learning Research Group at Xerox Palo Alto Research Center is concerned with all aspects
> of the communication and manipulation of knowledge. We design, build, and use dynamic media
> which can be used by human beings of all ages. Several years ago, we crystallized our dreams
> into a design idea for a personal dynamic medium the size of a notebook (the *Dynabook*) which
> could be owned by everyone and could have the power to handle virtually all of its owner's
> information-related needs."

Note on "children of all ages": that exact phrase is the title of Kay's earlier 1972 proposal,
*"A Personal Computer for Children of All Ages"* (ACM 1972), not literally present in the 1977
paper's text — the 1977 paper instead uses "human beings of all ages." Both papers describe the
same Dynabook vision; the 1972 title is the more famous formulation of "for children of all ages."

**What a medium is — active/simulative vs. passive, and the computer as metamedium:**
> "For most of recorded history, the interactions of humans with their media have been primarily
> nonconversational and passive in the sense that marks on paper, paint on walls, even 'motion'
> pictures and television, do not change in response to the viewer's wishes. A mathematical
> formulation — which may symbolize the essence of an entire universe — once put down on paper,
> remains static and requires the reader to expand its possibilities.
>
> Every message is, in one sense or another, a *simulation* of some idea. It may be
> representational or abstract. The essence of a medium is very much dependent on the way messages
> are embedded, changed, and viewed. Although digital computers were originally designed to do
> arithmetic computation, the ability to simulate the details of any descriptive model means that
> the computer, viewed as a medium itself, can be *all other media* if the embedding and viewing
> methods are sufficiently well provided. Moreover, this new 'metamedium' is *active* — it can
> respond to queries and experiments — so that the messages may involve the learner in a two-way
> conversation. This property has never been available before except through the medium of an
> individual teacher. We think the implications are vast and compelling."

This is the single clearest statement of Kay's medium concept: passive media (paper, paint, film,
TV) are fixed simulations that can't talk back; the computer is a medium that can *become* any
other medium and hold up its end of a two-way conversation — a "metamedium."

**The Dynabook vision itself (personal computer as a medium for thought):**
> "Imagine having your own self-contained knowledge manipulator in a portable package the size and
> shape of an ordinary notebook. Suppose it had enough power to outrace your senses of sight and
> hearing, enough capacity to store for later retrieval thousands of page-equivalents of reference
> materials, poems, letters, recipes, records, drawings, animations, musical scores, waveforms,
> dynamic simulations, and anything else you would like to remember and change."

**The flute metaphor — no discernible lag between cause and effect (why responsiveness matters
to a medium):**
> "There should be no discernible pause between cause and effect. One of the metaphors we used
> when designing such a system was that of a musical instrument, such as a flute, which is owned
> by its user and responds instantly and consistently to its owner's wishes. Imagine the absurdity
> of a one-second delay between blowing a note and hearing it!"

**"If the medium is the message... the message of low-bandwidth timesharing is 'blah'"** —
Kay's riff on McLuhan, contrasting the impoverishment of shared/timeshared computing power with
the abundance a personal dynamic medium needs:
> "These 'civilized' desires for flexibility, resolution, and response lead to the conclusion that
> a user of a dynamic personal medium needs several hundred times as much power as the average
> adult now typically enjoys from timeshared computing... If the 'medium is the message,' then the
> message of low-bandwidth timesharing is 'blah.'"

**Children as the user community, and why it matters that they can really program:**
> "Considering children as the users radiates a compelling excitement when viewed from a number of
> different perspectives. First, the children really can write programs that do serious things.
> Their programs use symbols to stand for objects, contain loops and recursions, require a fair
> amount of visualization of alternative strategies before a tactic is chosen, and involve
> interactive discovery and removal of 'bugs' in their ideas.
>
> The interactive nature of the dialogue, the fact that *they* are in control, the feeling that
> they are doing *real* things rather than playing with toys or working out 'assigned' problems,
> the pictorial and auditory nature of their results, all contribute to a tremendous sense of
> accomplishment."

**The book-as-medium reimagined (non-sequential, many paths through a narrative):**
> "A book can be read through the Dynabook: the memory can be inserted... It need not be treated
> as a simulated paper book since this is a new medium with new properties. A dynamic search may
> be made for a particular context. The non-sequential nature of the file medium and the use of
> dynamic manipulation allows a story to have many accessible points of view; Durrell's
> *Alexandria Quartet*, for instance, could be one book in which the reader may pursue many paths
> through the narrative."

**Conclusion — the Dynabook as a "metamedium" whose content is "a wide range of already-existing
and not-yet-invented media":**
> "What would happen in a world in which everyone had a Dynabook? If such a machine were designed
> in a way that *any* owner could mold and channel its power to his own needs, then a new kind of
> medium would have been created: a metamedium, whose content would be a wide range of
> already-existing and not-yet-invented media."

Examples given for what such a medium is *for* (i.e., thought, not just tasks): an architect
simulating and cross-referencing 3D designs, a doctor carrying his patients' records and a "drug
reaction system," a composer hearing a composition mid-progress and bypassing "the incredibly
tedious chore of recopying the score," children causing "exciting things to happen" with living
mathematics, poets editing and filing their own compositions.

### 2. "User Interface: A Personal View" (1989)

**Primary source, full text read:** In Brenda Laurel (ed.), *The Art of Human-Computer Interface
Design* (Addison-Wesley, 1990), pp. 191–207; reprinted in *The New Media Reader*, ch. 13,
pp. 121–131. PDFs: https://worrydream.com/refs/Kay_1989_-_User_Interface,_a_Personal_View.pdf ,
https://www.dgsiegel.net/files/refs/Kay%20-%20User%20Interface:%20A%20Personal%20View.pdf

**The computer-as-medium realization, via McLuhan:**
> "I read McLuhan's *Understanding Media* [1964] and understood that the most important thing
> about any communications medium is that message receipt is really message recovery; anyone who
> wishes to receive a message embedded in a medium must first have internalized the medium so it
> can be 'subtracted' out to leave the message behind. When he said 'the medium is the message' he
> meant that you have to *become* the medium if you use it... The computer is a medium! I had
> always thought of it as a tool, perhaps a vehicle — a much weaker conception."

**Why it must reach children, not wait for "driver's ed" in high school (medium vs. vehicle,
directly relevant to why UI design for children matters):**
> "If the computer is only a vehicle, perhaps you can wait until high school to give 'driver's ed'
> on it — but if it's a medium, then it must be extended all the way into the world of the child."

**Reading and writing a medium — literacy requires both:**
> "The ability to 'read' a medium means you can access materials and tools created by others. The
> ability to 'write' in a medium means you can *generate* materials and tools for others. You must
> have both to be literate. In print writing, the tools you generate are rhetorical; they
> demonstrate and convince. In computer writing, the tools you generate are processes; they
> simulate and decide."

**Naming the Dynabook (this is where Kay explains the name's origin, tying it explicitly to
McLuhan):**
> "What kind of a thinker would you become if you grew up with an active simulator connected, not
> just to one point of view, but to all the points of view of the ages represented so they could
> be dynamically tried out and compared? I named the notebook-sized computer idea the Dynabook to
> capture McLuhan's metaphor in the silicon to come."

**Bruner and the three mentalities — the direct lineage from Piaget through Bruner to Kay's
interface design:**
> "I discovered Jerome Bruner's *Towards a Theory of Instruction* [1966]. He had repeated and
> verified many of Piaget's results, and in the process came up with a different and much more
> powerful way to interpret what Piaget had seen... Bruner identified a separate mentality with
> each of Piaget's stages: he called them *enactive, iconic, symbolic*. While not ignoring the
> existence of other mentalities, he concentrated on these three to come up with what are still
> some of the strongest ideas for creating learning-rich environments.
>
> The work of Papert convinced me that whatever user interface design might be, it was solidly
> intertwined with learning. Bruner convinced me that learning takes place best environmentally
> and roughly in stage order — it is best to learn something kinesthetically, then iconically, and
> finally the intuitive knowledge will be in place that will allow the more powerful but less vivid
> symbolic processes to work at their strongest."

**Why you need all three modes, not just one — Kay's core interface-design thesis:**
> "Now, if we agree with the evidence that the human cognitive facilities are made up of a *doing*
> mentality, an *image* mentality, and a *symbolic* mentality, then any user interface we construct
> should at least cater to the mechanisms that seem to be there. But how? One approach is to
> realize that no single mentality offers a complete answer to the entire range of thinking and
> problem solving. User interface design should integrate them at least as well as Bruner did in
> his spiral curriculum ideas."

He also notes the mentalities *compete* rather than cooperate on their own, which is why the
interface has to force synergy deliberately:
> "One of the implications of the Piaget-Bruner decomposition is that the mentalities originated
> at very different evolutionary times and there is little probability that they can
> intercommunicate and synergize in more than the most rudimentary fashion. In fact, the
> mentalities are more likely to interfere with each other as they compete for control... because
> none of the mentalities is supremely useful to the exclusion of the others, the best strategy
> would be to try to gently force synergy between them in the user interface design."

**The three mentalities' jobs, as Kay tabulates them:**
> "enactive — know where you are, manipulate
> iconic — recognize, compare, configure, concrete
> symbolic — tie together long chains of reasoning, abstract"

**"Doing with Images makes Symbols" — the actual coining, and the mapping to GUI elements:**
> "Out of all this came the main slogan I coined to express this goal:
>
> **Doing with Images makes Symbols**
>
> The slogan also implies — as did Bruner — that one should start with — be grounded in — the
> concrete 'Doing with Images,' and be carried into the more abstract 'makes Symbols.'"

Kay's table mapping the slogan onto interface mechanisms and the Bruner modes (reproduced
verbatim from the text):
> "DOING → mouse → *enactive* → know where you are, manipulate
> with IMAGES → icons, windows → *iconic* → recognize, compare, configure, concrete
> makes SYMBOLS → Smalltalk → *symbolic* → tie together long chains of reasoning, abstract"

This is the direct answer to "why direct manipulation matters": manipulation via mouse/pointer is
how the enactive mentality gets engaged; icons/windows engage the iconic; the symbolic
(programming, language) sits on top and only works well once the other two have done their part —
which is also Kay's argument for why GUIs *had* to include direct manipulation, not as decoration
but as the necessary on-ramp to symbolic thought.

**The "user illusion" and theater — computer as a medium for thought, not just tasks.** Kay's own
essay doesn't use the exact phrase "user illusion" in the passages read here, but the concept is
attested in secondary sources describing his PARC-era thinking:
> "The user illusion is theatre, the ultimate mirror. It is the audience (the user) that is
> intelligent and can be directed into a particular context. Giving the audience the appropriate
> cues is the essence of user-interface design." (paraphrase/quote as reported in secondary
> literature on Kay's PARC work; the "lie that tells the truth" framing — the interface presents
> "a different kind of computer" that evokes in the user's mind what the machine can do — recurs
> across interviews. See discussion at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_illusion and
> https://morrick.me/archives/8824 (transcript of Kay's interview for the documentary *The Machine
> That Changed the World*, 1990) for corroborating material.)
> Confidence: **likely** (well-attested phrase and concept, but I could not pin an exact primary
> Kay text with page number for this specific wording in the time available — flagging as
> secondary-sourced).

**The essay's ending — "the best way to predict the future is to invent it" in its actual essay
context** (this is the primary-source context requested in point 4):
> "Well, there are so many more new issues that must be explored as well. I say thank goodness for
> that. How do we navigate in once-again uncharted waters? I have always believed that of all the
> ways to approach the future, the vehicle that gets you to the most interesting places is
> Romance. The notion of tool has always been a romantic idea to humankind — from swords to
> musical instruments to personal computers, it has been easy to say: 'The best way to predict the
> future is to invent it!' The romantic dream of 'how nice it would be if...' often has the power
> to bring the vision to life... As always, the strongest weapon we have to explore this new world
> is the one between our ears — providing it's loaded!"

### 3. "User illusion" and computer as medium for thinking, not task-tool

Covered above under §2. Additional framing, corroborated by multiple secondary sources: Kay and
colleagues at PARC used "user illusion" (with theatrical/stage-magic connotations) to describe
what a good interface does — it is a deliberately constructed, simplified, internally-consistent
fiction laid over a much more complex machine, calibrated to what's in the user's head rather than
what's literally happening in the hardware. This is the same throughline as "doing with images
makes symbols": the interface's job is to meet the several human mentalities where they are, not
to expose raw machine state.

**Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_illusion ;
https://medium.com/whmt/decoding-alan-kay-s-theory-on-software-c4e8b50d7108 ;
https://morrick.me/archives/8824 and https://morrick.me/archives/8833 (two-part transcript of
1990 interview).

### 4. "The best way to predict the future is to invent it" — origin and context

**Origin:** Kay himself has stated the line originated at an early-1970s PARC meeting.
> "The origin of the quote came from an early meeting in 1971 of PARC, Palo Alto Research Center,
> folks and the Xerox planners."
> — Kay, quoted via a 1998 email exchange, reported in Quote Investigator's sourcing:
> https://quoteinvestigator.com/2012/09/27/invent-the-future/

Earliest public documentation found: an April 1982 *InfoWorld* article describing it as "the
Xerox PARC researchers' maxim." Kay gave his clearest explanation of intended meaning in a 1983
*EDUCOM Bulletin* piece:
> "the best way to predict the future is to invent it, because we can then say, 'the future's
> there for us to shape — we're not helpless.'"
> — Kay, *EDUCOM Bulletin*, 1983 (as reported in Quote Investigator's sourcing above)

And, per Wikiquote, a 1984 paper titled *Inventing the Future* restates the same idea at length:
> "The future is not laid out on a track. It is something that we can decide, and to the extent
> that we do not violate any known laws of the universe, we can probably make it work the way that
> we want to."
> — https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Alan_Kay

I directly confirmed the line's appearance, in its full original rhetorical context (about
Romance, not mere prediction/planning), at the very end of "User Interface: A Personal View"
(1989) — quoted in full under §2 above. That is the clean primary-source citation: Kay uses the
line not as a chest-thumping slogan but as an example of "the vehicle that gets you to the most
interesting places" being "Romance" — the romantic dream of "how nice it would be if..." preceding
and motivating invention.

**Sources:**
- https://quoteinvestigator.com/2012/09/27/invent-the-future/
- https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Alan_Kay
- Kay, "User Interface: A Personal View" (1989), primary text (see §2)

### 5. Why most computing today fails to be a real medium — Kay's critiques

**On the Web being a "joke," done by amateurs (2012 interview):**
> "The Web, in comparison, is a joke. The Web was done by amateurs."
> — Alan Kay, Dr. Dobb's interview, 2012, quoted at https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Alan_Kay

**"Simple things should be simple, complex things should be possible"** — often cited as Kay's
compact statement of what most interfaces/toolkits fail to deliver (they make simple things
possible but not simple, or make complex things impossible). — https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Alan_Kay
Confidence: **confirmed** attribution per Wikiquote, exact original document/date not pinned in
this pass.

**On perspective and creativity** (relevant to why Kay thinks most software fails to be a medium
for *thought*, as opposed to task execution — a medium should let you change your point of view):
> "A change in perspective is worth 80 IQ points."
> — Alan Kay, talk at a Creative Think seminar, July 20, 1982, per
> https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Alan_Kay

**On the computer as tool vs. medium (his own diagnosis of the category error that keeps most
computing from being a real medium)**, repeated from §2 because it is the core of the critique:
> "The computer is a medium! I had always thought of it as a tool, perhaps a vehicle — a much
> weaker conception."
> — Kay, "User Interface: A Personal View" (1989)

**Note on the "tuning a piano to play one note" framing named in the task prompt:** I was not able
to find this specific formulation as an authenticated direct Kay quote in the sources checked
(WebSearch across several phrasings did not surface it verbatim; it appears in blog paraphrase
form in a few places but I could not trace an exact primary citation in the time available).
Flagging as **unconfirmed** — do not attribute this exact phrasing to Kay without further
verification against a primary transcript.

**Metamedium framing (a computer's job is to be an active simulator of "all other media"):**
> "the computer, viewed as a medium itself, can be *all other media* if the embedding and viewing
> methods are sufficiently well provided. Moreover, this new 'metamedium' is *active*..."
> — Kay & Goldberg, "Personal Dynamic Media" (1977), quoted in full under §1.

This is effectively Kay's standing critique of most modern computing: single-purpose apps (a word
processor, a browser tab, a chat app) each simulate only *one* medium and don't let the user
recombine, extend, or program them — the opposite of the metamedium vision, where the point of
having a Dynabook/personal computer is that its owner can "mold and channel its power to his own
needs."

**Additional secondary-sourced material worth citing on the metamedium point:**
> Kay defines the term "metamedium" by presenting the computer as an active simulator of all
> existing media and others yet to be established.
> — Helder Dias, "Metamedium (The Expanded Alan Kay)," ISEA2011 symposium archive:
> https://www.isea-symposium-archives.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/ISEA2011_115_Helder-Dias.pdf

## TED NELSON

### 1. Project Xanadu and transclusion

**Official Xanadu definition of transclusion** (from Xanadu project pages):
> "'Transclusion' is a term introduced by Ted Nelson to define virtual inclusion — the process of
> including something by reference rather than by copying... The Xanadu system must allow people
> to create virtual copies ('transclusions') of any existing collection of information in the
> system regardless of ownership... Anyone may quote anything in the Xanadu network by transclusion
> (virtual inclusion) from another publisher's document. The stable address of each portion to be
> quoted is put on the edit decision list [rather than copying the bytes]. This retains the
> connection to its original context."
> — https://www.aus.xanadu.com/xanadu/faq.html ; corroborated at
> https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfilade_(Xanadu) and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Xanadu

**Royalty/micropayment mechanism tied directly to transclusion:**
> "Every document can contain a royalty mechanism at any desired degree of granularity to ensure
> payment on any portion accessed, including virtual copies ('transclusions') of all or part of the
> document."
> — Xanadu documentation, quoted at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Xanadu ; see also
> https://xanadu.com/xuvision.html : "quoted contents are always delivered from the virtual
> original of the author or rightsholder," which is how Nelson's "transpublishing" pays a royalty
> per view/download without requiring negotiation.

**Nelson's own later restatement (1999), on why transclusion needs system-level support, not an
app-level bolt-on:**
> "We need to be able to recognize shared material, and see it side by side... transclusion —
> recognizing and seeing contents across such boundaries — is a literary concept... Transclusion
> must be system-supported, not added at the application level."
> — Ted Nelson, "Xanalogical Structure, Now More Than Ever" (1999),
> https://xanadu.net/NOWMORETHANEVER/XuSum99.html

**Nelson explicitly framing the Web as the betrayal of this idea:**
> "The World Wide Web was precisely what we were trying to PREVENT — ever-breaking links, links
> going outward only, quotes you can't follow to their origins, no version management, no rights
> management."
> — Ted Nelson, Ted Nelson's Home Page, November 17, 1998, quoted at
> https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Ted_Nelson

> "The Web achieves the flashy, easy-to-do 50% of any problem," while leaving out version
> management, rights management, and principled content reuse — the harder half Xanadu was built
> to solve.
> — paraphrase of Nelson's critique, sourced from
> https://xanadu.net/NOWMORETHANEVER/XuSum99.html

### 2. "Intertwingularity" — exact quote and context

**Full original passage, Computer Lib/Dream Machines (1974), p. DM45 (per Wikipedia's sourcing,
cross-checked against a blog post that traced the exact original vs. 1987-revised-edition
wording):**
> "EVERYTHING IS DEEPLY INTERTWINGLED. In an important sense there are no 'subjects' at all; there
> is only all knowledge, since the cross-connections among the myriad topics of this world simply
> cannot be divided up neatly."
> — Ted Nelson, *Computer Lib/Dream Machines* (1974), p. 42 (Computer Lib side) / p. 45 (Dream
> Machines side). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertwingularity ; cross-checked against
> https://hyperorg.com/blogger/2007/06/09/what-ted-nelson-actually-said-about-intertwingularity

**The follow-on sentence about hierarchy, added in the 1987 revised edition (p. 31) — this is the
part that most directly states "hierarchical/sequential systems falsify" reality, and per the
hyperorg.com fact-check it is worth being precise that this exact sentence is from the *1987*
revision, not the 1974 original, even though it's commonly quoted as a single continuous passage:**
> "Hierarchical and sequential structures, especially popular since Gutenberg, are usually forced
> and artificial. Intertwingularity is not generally acknowledged — people keep pretending they can
> make things hierarchical, categorizable and sequential when they can't."
> — Ted Nelson, *Computer Lib/Dream Machines*, rev. ed. 1987, p. 31. Source:
> https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertwingularity ; fact-checked at
> https://hyperorg.com/blogger/2007/06/09/what-ted-nelson-actually-said-about-intertwingularity
> (which explicitly warns that the commonly circulated "combined" quote splices the 1974 and 1987
> texts together, and that the widely-repeated version even drops the word "deeply.")

**Confidence note:** the single most quoted three words — "everything is deeply intertwingled" —
are confirmed 1974-original (both Computer Lib and Dream Machines sides, printed twice, since the
book was bound with two front covers to literally embody its own thesis:
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_Lib/Dream_Machines). The hierarchy-falsifies-reality

### 3. Parallel visible links — Nelson's critique of today's hyperlinks vs. his original vision

**Nelson's blunt statement of the difference (Web = one-way, embedded, invisible-until-followed;
Xanadu = two-way, visible, non-destructive):**
> "The Web has only one-way links and no provision for maintaining them as documents change. The
> Xanadu concept... permits two-way links which stay in place as documents change and move."
> — Ted Nelson, https://xanadu.net/NOWMORETHANEVER/XuSum99.html

**A more technical framing of the same point, from a paper analyzing Xanadu vs. WWW (quoting the
structural distinction directly, note this passage is the paper author's own words characterizing
Nelson's design, not a Nelson quote — flagged accordingly):**
> Web links "are embedded within the documents themselves and are unidirectional and univisible.
> That is, they can only be followed in one direction and can only be seen from the originating
> end," whereas Xanadu's design calls for "bivisible and bifollowable links" with external
> metadata.
> — Andrew Pam et al., discussion of Xanadu vs. WWW, https://www.aus.xanadu.com/archive/6w-paper.html
> (paraphrase of Nelson's design position, not a direct Nelson quote — the source page is
> co-authored by Xanadu Australia collaborators, not solely Nelson).

**Nelson's own description of the 1972 "parallel visible links" visualization — links you can see
in context, without navigating away:**
> Nelson describes a 1972 design where "connections between contents of windows" remain visible
> "as content scrolls or the windows move," adding: "I believe they are needed everywhere, in
> virtually every activity."
> — Ted Nelson, https://xanadu.net/NOWMORETHANEVER/XuSum99.html

**On being able to see the source of every quotation, and compare documents side by side (the
heart of "parallel documents" as opposed to a single-pane browser tab that replaces the origin
with the destination):**
> "Being able to work with parallel documents, and see them connected side by side, is crucial."
> — Ted Nelson, https://xanadu.com/xuvision.html

> The original Xanadu vision proposed "an entire form of literature where links do not break as
> versions change; where documents may be closely compared side by side and closely annotated;
> where it is possible to see the origins of every quotation; and in which there is a valid
> copyright system."
> — paraphrase of Nelson's stated Xanadu goals, cross-referenced at
> https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Xanadu and https://xanadu.net/NOWMORETHANEVER/XuSum99.html

**Nelson calling Berners-Lee's links a dumbed-down version of his own idea:**
> "After all, dumbing down Xanadu sure worked well for Tim Berners-Lee!"
> — Ted Nelson, "Indirect Documents at Last!" (2005), quoted at
> https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Ted_Nelson

> On the windowing/hypertext interfaces that shipped instead of his design: "computer windows as we
> presently see them were designed after this proposal, and represent a significant dumbdown of
> human capability."
> — Ted Nelson, https://xanadu.com/xuvision.html

### 4. Critique of hierarchy and files/folders as a bad metaphor

**The "prison guard / counselor" one-liner — renaming a hierarchical directory doesn't change what
it is:**
> "Calling a hierarchical directory a 'folder' doesn't change its nature any more than calling a
> prison guard a 'counselor'."
> — Ted Nelson, "Ted's Computer Paradigm, Expressed as One-Liners,"
> https://xanadu.com.au/ted/TN/WRITINGS/TCOMPARADIGM/tedCompOneLiners.html (also mirrored at
> http://hyperland.com/TedCompOneLiners)

**On hierarchical directories being a dated, non-scaling fix:**
> "Hierarchical directories were invented around 1947... It's a temporary fix that doesn't scale
> up."
> — Ted Nelson, same source as above.

**On the "file" concept itself as an impoverished representation:**
> "A file is a large lump of data with a fixed name and location, whose contents may or may not
> change."
> — Ted Nelson, same source (stated as a criticism of the concept's rigidity relative to how
> ideas/documents actually evolve).

**The 747-as-a-bus line, on WYSIWYG/paper simulation as a category error for computers:**
> "Imitating paper on a computer screen is like tearing the wings off a 747 and using it as a
> bus [on the highway]."
> — Ted Nelson, quoted at https://xanadu.com.au/ted/TN/WRITINGS/TCOMPARADIGM/tedCompOneLiners.html
> and corroborated in secondary discussion at https://coolguy.website/hero-ted-nelson/

**On paper's linearity as a "prison," with footnotes as a symptom of ideas straining against it —
this is Nelson's clearest articulation of *why* hierarchy/sequence falsifies how ideas relate:**
> Nelson described paper as **"a prison,"** with footnotes representing "hands reaching out of
> jailhouse windows" — ideas trying to escape a form (the linear page) that cannot represent their
> actual, non-linear interconnections.
> — reported in secondary discussion, https://www.openpolitics.com/articles/ted-nelson-philosophy-of-hypertext.html
> Confidence: **likely** (consistent phrasing appears in multiple secondary write-ups of Nelson's
> talks/interviews; I was not able to pin this to a single primary transcript with a date in the
> time available for this pass).

**On why he wanted to eliminate both hierarchy and the paper metaphor at once, and his objection to
what Xerox PARC actually shipped:**
> Nelson's goal was to "eliminate both the hierarchical folders and the metaphor of paper," and he
> objected especially to the GUI/WYSIWYG editor developed at Xerox PARC, which "laid the foundation
> of our paper simulation paradigm."
> — reported in secondary discussion, https://coolguy.website/hero-ted-nelson/ (cf.
> https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=7126802 , "Ted Nelson: It All Went Wrong at Xerox PARC")

**On regularity/hierarchy being imposed where it doesn't belong (ZigZag talk, 2008):**
> "We should not impose regularity where it does not exist."
> — Ted Nelson, talk on ZigZag data structures, YouTube, September 6, 2008, quoted at
> https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Ted_Nelson

**On archives/historical filing specifically resisting the temptation toward false hierarchy —
directly on point for "files/folders as a bad metaphor for how ideas actually relate":**
> "I hope that in our archives and historical filings of the future, we do not allow the techie
> traditions of hierarchy and false regularity to be superimposed [on] the teeming, fantastic
> disorderliness of human life."
> — Ted Nelson, quoted (source: raphkoster.com article) at
> https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Ted_Nelson
